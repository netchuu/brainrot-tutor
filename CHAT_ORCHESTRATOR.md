## Can you build it? Yes, and two days is enough for a demo

The video in your screenshot isn't AI-generated video. It's layers put together on a timeline, and a script can describe every layer:

| Layer in your example | What it is technically |
|---|---|
| Minecraft parkour background | One looping gameplay clip, dimmed |
| "Brain" image at the top | One generated or stock image per scene |
| `CAR'S BRAIN` / `EVEN POSSIBLE?` | 2–3 words on screen at a time, one in an accent color, timed to each spoken word |
| Peter and Stewie | Static cut-out images that switch depending on who is speaking. It's a dialogue: a teacher and a skeptical student |
| 😨 emoji, "?" | Reaction stickers |

So the pipeline is: **file → AI writes a scene script (JSON) → voices → word timings → assemble → play.** Video-generation models like Veo or Sora are optional and not needed.

## What already exists

There's a whole category called **"PDF to Brainrot"**, and it took off as a TikTok trend in late 2024:
- **Revid, Hooked, EasyBrainrot ($9/mo Pro), pdftobrainrot, StudyAura, Braintok (iOS):** you upload a PDF or text, AI narrates it over Minecraft or Subway Surfers footage, with word-by-word captions. pdftobrainrot also has a "Quiz Mode."
- **StudyFetch, Coconote, StudyRot, Memenome:** study apps with the same feature (per TechCrunch).
- **Google NotebookLM:** Video Overviews, plus flashcards and quizzes generated from your sources. It's polished but not in brainrot style, and the "Cinematic" videos are a paid tier.

**Where they fall short:** almost all of them stop once the video ends. TechCrunch's main criticism is accuracy: the AI rewrites the source freely and can get things wrong. I found no study showing these tools help people learn. Your plan (video, then tests, practice problems, and a tutor) covers exactly that gap.

## Open source you can borrow from

- **[gyoridavid/short-video-maker](https://github.com/gyoridavid/short-video-maker)** (MIT, 1.4k★): the closest blueprint. It does Kokoro voice → whisper.cpp captions → Pexels clips → Remotion. Its voice is English-only, so you'd swap that part.
- **[harry0703/MoneyPrinterTurbo](https://github.com/harry0703/MoneyPrinterTurbo)** (MIT, ~135k★, active): a Python pipeline that takes a topic and produces a short video, with a web UI and an API.
- **[elebumm/RedditVideoMakerBot](https://github.com/elebumm/RedditVideoMakerBot)** (12.5k★): the classic voice-over-gameplay bot. It's **GPL-3.0**, so copying its code would make your project GPL too.
- **brainrot.js (noahgsolomon):** the best-known "learn any topic from your favorite characters" project, built on Remotion. Its GitHub repo now returns 404, so only third-party descriptions of it are left.

## Recommended stack

| Layer | Pick | Why |

## Legal: fine for a demo, a problem for a product

- **Family Guy characters and cloned voices** are Fox/Disney property, and voice-clone lawsuits are active. For the hackathon, use your own mascot characters (Nano Banana keeps them consistent) and stock ElevenLabs voices.
- **Minecraft:** Mojang's guidelines allow gameplay videos, including monetized ones, but restrict using them to promote products. Record your own parkour footage. I didn't check Subway Surfers' policy, so I'd avoid it.

## How to pitch it

- **Research on "seductive details"** (meta-analysis, 72 comparisons): eye-catching but unrelated material tends to *hurt* learning. So keep the gameplay as a dimmed background and put the actual content (diagram, formula, key term) in the center.
- **Retrieval practice** is among the best-supported findings in education research: 222 studies with 48,478 students, and quizzes work better with feedback.
- Pitch line: **"Brainrot gets their attention, practice makes it stick, and every fact is tied to your source."**

## Main risks

1. **MP4 render time.** brainrot.js reportedly warned of 10–20 minutes per video. Playing in the browser with the Player avoids this for the demo.
2. **Getting captions in sync** with the voice.
3. **Kyrgyz voice quality.** ElevenLabs supports it, and there are open-source options (KaniTTS, AkylAI), but I haven't tested any of them.
4. **AI mistakes in the script.** Every line should come from the uploaded material.
5. **Remotion license** if your team has 4 or more people.

## What "free" costs you instead

1. **Rate limits.** Each video takes about 3–5 AI calls (script, quiz, a few chat turns). Twenty requests a day on the newest Gemini Flash won't last through a hackathon, so use Flash-Lite for most calls and Groq as a backup.
2. **Reliability.** edge-tts and Pollinations have no guarantees. Before the demo, generate a few videos ahead of time and cache them.
3. **Quality.** Free voices sound more like a narrator than a meme. The captions, cut-outs and fast pace carry most of the "brainrot" feel anyway.
4. **Kyrgyz** is the weakest part. If it matters for your pitch, test KaniTTS early on day 1.

## Short answer: users never have to wait for a render

The 10–20 minute waits in tools like brainrot.js come from rendering a finished MP4 on a server before showing anything. Skip that. **The browser plays the layers live, like a game,** and the content is generated scene by scene while the first scene is already playing. The target is **video starting in about 5–10 seconds**, all on free services.

## What I measured on this Mac (Apple M5)

| Step | Result | Usable live? |
|---|---|---|
| Free voice, 1 line (6.9 s of speech) | 1.7–3.9 s to generate | ✅ |
| Free voice, **6 lines in parallel** (31 s of speech) | **3.3 s total** | ✅ |
| Free AI image, no key (Pollinations) | 8.8 s for one image, then blocked: *"1 image every 45 seconds per IP"*, plus a watermark | ❌ Keep it out of the live flow |
| **MP4 export with FFmpeg**: 30 s, 1080×1920, background, image, captions, character, voice | **3.2–4.0 s**, even limited to 2 CPU threads | ✅ Only on Download |
| AI script (Gemini / Groq) | Not measured, since it needs API keys | My estimate: a few seconds |

I checked a frame from the exported MP4, and every layer rendered correctly, including the Russian captions. A free cloud server is slower than an M5, so expect the export to take several times longer there. That's a guess, not a measurement.

## How the fast version works

```
0 s     user uploads a file
~2–4 s  AI streams scene 1 of the script (scenes arrive one by one)
~5–7 s  scene 1 voice ready → PLAY starts
        scenes 2…N: voice generated while the earlier ones play
        (~3 s to generate vs ~5 s of speech per scene, so it stays ahead)
        quiz generated in parallel → ready when the video ends
```

The times above are my estimates.

1. **Don't render in order to view.** Use Remotion Player, or plain HTML/CSS layers with an `<audio>` element and timed captions.
2. **Stream the script** from the AI one scene at a time, and start that scene's voice as soon as it arrives.
3. **Run everything in parallel:** voice for all scenes at once, quiz at the same time.
4. **Keep slow parts out of the live flow.** Instead of AI images, use a fast stock image search (Pexels), emoji, and a pre-made sticker and character library. AI images can be swapped in later if they finish in time.
5. **Prepare assets ahead of time:** background loops, character cut-outs and sound effects, all loaded from a CDN.
6. **Make an MP4 only when someone taps Download.** Either in the browser (Remotion's web renderer, which its docs call "very experimental", or Mediabunny) or on the server with FFmpeg, which takes about 4 s for a 30 s video on this Mac.
7. **Cache results by file,** so the same file gives an instant replay. Also pre-generate a couple of videos before the demo.

## Free hosting

- **Vercel Hobby (free): the whole app.** The website plus API functions that call Gemini/Groq and the voice service. Functions can run up to 5 minutes, and each call needs only seconds. Keep API keys in these server functions, never in the browser.
- **Avoid Render's free tier for the live path.** It goes to sleep after 15 minutes idle and takes 30–60 seconds to wake up, which is bad on stage.
- **Hugging Face Spaces** (2 vCPU / 16 GB free) is only needed for server-side MP4 export. Its docs conflict on whether Docker Spaces are still free, so check before relying on it. In-browser export avoids the question.

## What will actually slow you down

1. **Free-tier rate limits** (errors when you exceed quotas), not computing power. Switch automatically between Gemini and Groq when one fails.
2. **The voice service is flaky:** in my test, the male Russian voice failed on some sentences. Retry once, then fall back to the female voice with a different pitch.
3. **Venue Wi-Fi:** have pre-generated demo videos ready.

Yes, use templates. Your example video is made exactly this way, and it's also the fastest and cheapest option. **People make the templates once. For each video, the AI only fills in the empty "slots" in a template.** The AI never draws any video itself.

## What's ready-made and what the AI does

| Ready-made (done once by your team) | AI fills in per video |
|---|---|
| Background clips (parkour, satisfying videos, cooking…) | The script: short scenes and who speaks each line |
| Character clips or cut-outs (green-screen or PNG) | Caption text and which word gets the highlight color |
| Reaction stickers, emoji, sound effects (whoosh, vine boom) | Which picture goes at the top, picked from stock search |
| Layout: where the picture, captions and character sit | Which template, which reaction, and when |
| Fonts, colors, transitions | Voice (free TTS) and the quiz afterwards |

The AI returns something small like this, and the template engine does the rest:

```json
{"template": "parkour_duo", "scenes": [
  {"speaker": "teacher", "text": "ECU is the car's brain", "highlight": "brain",
   "picture": "car engine computer", "reaction": "mind_blown", "sfx": "whoosh"}]}
```

## I tested it

I made a fake green-screen character clip, removed the green, and placed it over a gameplay background with a top picture, timed captions and voice. **30 s of 1080×1920 video rendered in 6.5 s**, limited to 2 CPU threads on this Mac. The green removal worked cleanly. One thing I learned: long Russian words overflowed the caption area, so captions need to shrink to fit automatically (or the AI should be told to keep them short).

It's fast because everything heavy is already made. The only per-video work is a few seconds of AI text and voice, and the browser can play the result right away.

## Free tools that assemble templates

- **Remotion** (React, 66.8k★): the most polished option. Free for teams of up to 3.
- **Revideo** (MIT, active): templates written in TypeScript, a browser player that takes your inputs, and a render endpoint on the server.
- **Editly** (MIT, 5.5k★): closest to "template = JSON file". It handles text, transparent images, clips with transparency and audio mixing on top of FFmpeg. Its last update was May 2025, so it's less actively maintained.
- **FFmpeg / MoviePy:** lowest level, which is what I used for the test.
- Paid template services with visual editors (Creatomate, JSON2Video, Shotstack) exist, but their free tiers are tiny or watermarked, so you don't need them.

## Where to get template clips safely

- **Pexels videos:** free for commercial use, no attribution, free API. **Pixabay:** editing allowed. **Mixkit / Videvo green-screen clips:** check each clip's license.
- **Make your own:** record gameplay in Luanti, and film your team's reactions in front of a green sheet. It's the safest option and a good story for the pitch.
- **GIFs:** Google shut down the Tenor API on June 30, 2026. GIPHY is the main replacement, but I couldn't confirm its free limits or attribution rules.
- **Avoid** downloaded meme packs with TV show clips (Family Guy and the like). The footage still belongs to its owners.

**One downside:** with only a couple of templates, every video looks the same. Make about 5–10 templates and let the AI vary the background section, reactions, colors and transitions so each video feels new.
